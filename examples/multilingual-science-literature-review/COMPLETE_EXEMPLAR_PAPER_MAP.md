# Language and Participation in Secondary Science Inquiry

> **Selected-source teaching exemplar.** The four publications cited in this example are genuine. Three are education publications used for a bounded conceptual comparison, and the fourth is a review-reporting guideline. They were assembled as a teaching set. No historical database-search results, screening log, registered protocol, or empirical effect estimates are supplied. This is not a conducted systematic or scoping review, and its statements must not be read as exhaustive findings about multilingual students.

## Research question

What considerations for language and participation in secondary science investigation can be drawn from three selected education publications, and what limits their interpretation?

## Introduction and scholarly context

When students investigate a scientific question, they need opportunities to interpret evidence and explain what their observations may mean. The language of science is involved in these activities, alongside other forms of representation and participation. The experience can be especially varied for learners who use more than one language, although the available publications do not describe one uniform group or educational setting.

Lee, Quinn and Valdés (2013) discuss the language demands and opportunities embedded in science and engineering practices, with particular attention to English language learners. Their account places language within the work of investigating and communicating scientific ideas. The National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (2018) examine English learners' STEM experiences across a broader educational landscape. That consensus report considers the linguistic resources students bring to classrooms and the conditions that shape access to rigorous learning.

A separate National Academies report (2019) centres investigation and engineering design in Grades 6–12. It addresses the learning opportunities created when students work with phenomena, evidence and explanations, rather than focusing specifically on multilingual students. Reading these three publications together raises a question about how language and participation might be considered when secondary science teaching centres inquiry. Their relationship is an interpretation developed in this miniature reading exercise, not a shared empirical outcome established across three comparable trials.

## Method and source selection

### Scope and provenance

The example uses a purposively chosen set of **three substantive education publications** and **one methodological reporting reference**. The education sources consist of a conceptual scholarly article and two National Academies consensus reports. The remaining source is Page et al. (2021), the PRISMA 2020 statement. It concerns the transparent reporting of systematic reviews and is excluded from conclusions about science-language experiences.

The repository contains the four references and a short source matrix. It does **not** contain a dated database search, exported retrieval results, deduplication log, eligibility criteria applied to a search yield, rejected-record explanations, a protocol or independent appraisal of underlying studies. It would be misleading to construct a PRISMA flow diagram from the four selected records or to imply that these publications are the survivors of documented comprehensive screening.

A future systematic review could build concept blocks using terms for multilingual learners, English learners, science practices, investigation and disciplinary language. Reviewers would then need to save the exact searches, dates, restrictions and records, track screening decisions and apply an appropriate synthesis and appraisal plan. Here those steps are described as **prospective methodological considerations**, not procedures already carried out.

### Approach to synthesis

The teaching analysis first considers what the three education sources address separately. It then explores where their ideas may inform a common question about participation in investigation. Differences in population and scope remain visible throughout. The reporting guideline is used only to explain why a more comprehensive review would need to document its methods.

The resulting account is conceptual and interpretive. It cannot estimate the prevalence of a finding in the research literature, assess the effectiveness of a particular classroom scaffold, or assert convergence across systematically identified studies.

## Findings from the selected publications

### Language within scientific practices

The 2013 article locates demands and opportunities for language use within science and engineering practices. Scientific explanation involves more than selecting correct terminology; students may need to communicate relationships and make the basis of their reasoning clear. The article invites attention to language as part of disciplinary participation, particularly when students are learning English while engaging with science.

This is a conceptual contribution from **LR-S01**. The small teaching set does not determine which classroom language supports produce better learning outcomes, nor does the article supply an effect estimate that could answer such a question here.

### Students' resources and access to STEM learning

Students identified as English learners bring linguistic, cultural and experiential resources to STEM classrooms. The 2018 National Academies consensus report discusses those resources alongside opportunities for sustained participation in rigorous content learning. It encourages consideration of the educational settings and instructional conditions through which students encounter STEM.

The relevant publication is **LR-S02**. Its focus on English learners should be reported precisely. A multilingual student is not necessarily classified as an English learner, and the report does not establish an identical experience for every person who uses multiple languages. Its recommendations also cannot be reduced to an effect measured in one local class.

### Investigation and evidence-based participation

In Grades 6–12 science, investigation and design can provide settings in which students develop questions, work with evidence and communicate explanatory ideas. The 2019 National Academies consensus report examines why placing these practices at the centre of instruction is valuable and what implementation requires.

**LR-S03** provides this broader disciplinary context. It does not specifically estimate how multilingual participation changes when a teacher adopts an investigation-based lesson. Bringing its account together with the first two publications is therefore a reasoned connection between their topics. It should remain distinguishable from a claim directly tested across the three publications.

## Discussion and limitations

The selected readings draw attention to a relationship worth examining in science education. Students' opportunities to investigate may depend partly on the forms of communication through which evidence becomes discussable, and their existing linguistic experiences may offer resources for that work. The three publications approach these questions from different starting points. Treating their differences carefully can make the conceptual discussion more useful than simply announcing that every source agrees.

At the same time, this example cannot establish a recurring empirical principle in the field. Its sources were selected for teaching, without an archived search procedure or explicit decisions about alternative publications. The two consensus reports already contain reviews and interpretations of wider evidence, while the 2013 article offers a conceptual analysis of science practices. Their number tells us nothing about the frequency of findings in primary studies.

The categories of learners and contexts also require care. English learners in the cited sources are not identical to the wider population of multilingual students. The Grades 6–12 investigation report addresses all learners rather than this specific population. Further empirical work would be needed to examine particular instructional opportunities, language practices and scientific explanations in defined secondary classrooms. This teaching corpus cannot establish either the absence or the presence of such work elsewhere.

PRISMA 2020 supplies a further methodological reminder. Transparent systematic-review reporting describes how studies were identified, selected, appraised and synthesised. A four-reference matrix and an appealing conceptual discussion cannot substitute for those records. Since this exercise was not a systematic review, a PRISMA flowchart would overstate what was done.

## Conclusion

Three selected education publications offer complementary considerations for understanding language and participation in secondary science inquiry. They address the language involved in scientific practices, the resources and STEM opportunities of English learners, and the value of working with evidence through investigation and design. These relationships can guide further questions. They are not comprehensive findings or intervention estimates established by this selected-source teaching exercise.

The fourth publication contributes guidance about reporting genuine reviews. Keeping its methodological purpose separate from the three education sources helps preserve the distinction between **a documented review process** and **a deliberately limited scholarly reading exercise**.
