# Multilingual Students Explaining Data-Rich Biology Questions

> **Synthetic teaching exemplar.** All eight speaker identifiers, statements, initial codes and candidate thematic groups are invented. The references are genuine scholarly publications. No actual students were interviewed, and the material contains no full transcripts, recruitment records, consent documentation or classroom observations.

## Research question

How do multilingual secondary students describe the challenges and supports they experience when explaining data-rich biology questions in English?

## Introduction and literature basis

Students who explain a biological pattern must consider what the data show and decide how to communicate a relationship. The language used in science can make aspects of reasoning available to others, while different forms of participation can offer students ways to develop and express an idea. Lee, Quinn and Valdés (2013) examined language demands and opportunities embedded in science and engineering practices. Their work provides a reason to attend to communication within disciplinary activity rather than treating English wording as a direct measure of conceptual knowledge.

The National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (2018) also describe the resources English learners bring to STEM learning and the importance of opportunities to participate. That report addresses a particular population of learners classified as learning English; its terms cannot simply be applied to every multilingual student. Taken together, the sources support questions about participation and the languages students use in science. They do not establish what any individual student in the proposed setting would say or understand.

An interview study could explore how students themselves describe this work. Braun and Clarke (2006) offer methodological guidance for thematic analysis, including the choices and interpretations involved in developing patterns of meaning. The present material demonstrates only a small part of such work through invented excerpts and a provisional code grouping.

## Method

### Proposed qualitative design and synthetic corpus

The hypothetical research design is qualitative and interview-oriented. In an actual project, semi-structured conversations might ask students to describe how they understand data-rich biology tasks, prepare explanations and respond to opportunities to use different languages or representations. Follow-up questions would be necessary to understand what participants meant and how their descriptions relate to particular classroom situations.

The supplied teaching file, `SYNTHETIC_INTERVIEW_EXCERPTS.csv`, contains **eight fabricated statements**, one for each fictional participant ID from P01 through P08. Its three fields are `participant_id`, `excerpt` and `initial_code`. Neither the prompts that elicited the supposed statements nor their wider conversational contexts exist. Each row is therefore an isolated invented example, not a transcript of a completed interview.

### Ethical and interpretive considerations

If this research were carried out with real students, researchers would need appropriate school access and any required ethics review, assent or consent, and parental permission where applicable. A teacher would need to avoid making research participation appear connected to assessment or grades. Students should have meaningful ways to decline and, where feasible, to express their experiences in languages that allow them to communicate comfortably.

Protecting confidentiality would involve more than replacing names with participant codes. Details about a small school or a distinctive language history could still make a student recognisable. Researchers would also need to explain how recordings, translation, transcription and quotation permissions would be handled. None of these procedures was implemented for the fictional excerpt file.

### Coding and candidate theme construction

The CSV supplies an initial code for each of the eight statements. The separate `SYNTHETIC_THEME_MATRIX.json` groups those codes into three provisional headings and identifies the exact excerpt IDs supporting each one. The matrix was checked and revised to include the code for **P02**, which was previously omitted. Its remaining headings were refined to respect differences in the statements and the uneven support across groupings.

The exercise illustrates how an analyst can move from exact excerpt wording to initial codes and then to possible patterns. It does not reproduce the iterative engagement with complete transcripts, contextual case material and interpretive development that a full empirical thematic study would require. Code counts are used here only to show which invented statements are grouped together, not as estimates of how common the experiences are.

## Illustrative findings

**Every quotation in this section is fictional and reproduced exactly from the teaching CSV.** The candidate groupings are interpretations of these constructed statements.

### 1. English wording and task interpretation shape visible explanations

Four excerpts point towards related but distinct concerns. P01 describes waiting while formulating an English sentence, whereas P02 refers to the wording of the question obscuring the biology idea. P05 asks what form of response is expected, and P06 distinguishes handling numerical information from explaining a pattern.

> **P01, fictional excerpt.** “I usually understand the graph first, but I wait because I need time to make the English sentence sound correct.”

> **P02, fictional excerpt.** “When the question has many words, I forget which biology idea the graph is testing.”

> **P05, fictional excerpt.** “Sometimes I know the answer but I am not sure if the teacher wants a full sentence or just the evidence.”

> **P06, fictional excerpt.** “Numbers are easier than explaining why the pattern happens.”

These invented speakers describe different moments in the work of responding to a task. Their statements would not allow a researcher to determine actual language proficiency or scientific understanding. In particular, uncertainty about the expected format of an answer need not be equivalent to difficulty understanding its biological content.

### 2. Visual, peer and multilingual resources are described as helpful

Three further statements describe resources the fictional speakers say they draw on while preparing an explanation. P03 mentions drawing arrows, P04 describes discussing an idea with a partner, and P07 describes using Korean in notes before producing an English explanation.

> **P03, fictional excerpt.** “Drawing arrows helps me explain before I find the exact English word.”

> **P04, fictional excerpt.** “If I can discuss with a partner first, I know what I want to say in English.”

> **P07, fictional excerpt.** “I use Korean in my notes, then I translate the science idea after I understand it.”

These examples suggest questions about the different routes through which students might work with and communicate scientific ideas. They do not demonstrate that a particular resource improved scientific reasoning or that all multilingual students would use it in the same way. In a real study, relevant classroom artefacts or interactional records could allow the researcher to examine what students did as well as what they later described.

### 3. Sentence starters alongside students' evidence choices

The final grouping contains only P08. That statement combines an account of faster responding with the wish to retain a role in selecting the scientific evidence.

> **P08, fictional excerpt.** “When the teacher gives sentence starters, I answer faster, but I still want to choose my own evidence.”

This may be a useful issue for subsequent interviews. The one invented statement cannot establish a recurring theme across actual participants, and the reported faster response was not independently measured. A fuller qualitative study could explore when language scaffolds support participation and when students experience them as limiting their choices.

## Discussion and limitations

The three groupings illustrate how a qualitative interpretation can become more precise when the analyst keeps the source statements visible. The first grouping gathers several forms of difficulty without treating them as one deficiency. The second directs attention towards representational and linguistic resources that speakers might use. The third invites a question about how support and student agency can coexist, while retaining its much narrower evidential basis.

Science-language scholarship provides concepts for discussing these possibilities. The synthetic excerpts, however, cannot establish that any student experienced them. Nor can the simplified grouping show that a genuine thematic analysis was completed. There is no evidence of the fictional speakers' backgrounds, the questions asked, translation practices, analytic memos, discrepant-case exploration, follow-up conversations or observed explanations.

A more complete study would need to examine the local context and researcher position carefully. An interview statement might reflect a particular task, a relationship with the teacher, expectations about assessment or a student's interpretation of the researcher's question. Analysis of full accounts, rather than excerpts selected in advance, could alter the proposed grouping substantially.

The most useful lesson from this exercise is the difference between a plausible reading of a statement and an empirically warranted account of students' experiences. Preserving the exact words and documenting each interpretive step helps make that difference visible. It cannot replace the evidence and ethical procedures of an actual study.

## Conclusion

The eight fictional statements support an instructional exercise in coding and provisional theme construction. All eight original codes are now traceable to source excerpts, and the single-excerpt third grouping is identified as tentative. These examples can help readers consider how multilingual students might describe science explanations, while making no finding about learners in any real classroom.
