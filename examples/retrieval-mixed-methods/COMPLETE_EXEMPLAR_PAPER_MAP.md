# Retrieval Practice, Score Change, and Student Experience

> **Synthetic instructional exemplar.** The three publications cited below are authentic scholarly sources. The biology scores, interview excerpts, codes, statistical findings and integrated interpretation in this report are constructed for teaching. They do not describe research conducted with students.

## Research question

How did biology scores change during a retrieval-practice routine, how did students describe the routine, and how do the quantitative and qualitative findings jointly inform interpretation?

## Introduction

When students practise retrieving information, the activity can offer opportunities to revisit material and reveal uncertainty in what they recall. Experimental research provides a reason to investigate these activities. Roediger and Karpicke (2006) compared retrieval testing with additional study and examined retention across different delays. Karpicke and Blunt (2011) compared retrieval practice with elaborative studying through concept mapping and found an advantage for retrieval under the conditions examined in their study. The studies differ in their instructional activities and outcome assessments. Their findings cannot be transferred directly to an unspecified biology classroom routine.

A classroom score change, considered alone, leaves important questions about students' experiences unanswered. Students may find that attempting a response clarifies what they need to study, or they may experience repetition and uncertainty about unfamiliar questions. Johnson and Onwuegbuzie (2004) explain the rationale for bringing quantitative and qualitative methods together in a research design. For this teaching example, the two strands address different parts of the question, and the shared student identifiers allow an explicit comparison. No published study supplies the fictional data presented below.

## Method and evidence

The constructed dataset contains 24 student-level records labelled M01 through M24. Each has a pre-score, a post-score and a calculated difference, defined as post-score minus pre-score. The scores are recorded to one decimal place. They are treated as score points because no assessment scale or measurement instrument is documented. No records establish the duration of the retrieval routine, comparability of the pre- and post-assessments, classroom context or sampling from a population.

A second file contains six fictional interview excerpts, each with a student identifier and a supplied initial code. The identifiers M03, M07, M09, M12, M18 and M21 also occur in the quantitative file. There are no full interview transcripts, interview questions, documented sampling criteria or independent coding records. Accordingly, the qualitative component is a transparent demonstration of organising brief accounts rather than an empirical thematic analysis.

The instructional workflow begins by analysing the paired differences, then considers the six excerpts in their original wording, and finally places each excerpt alongside its linked score change. The order describes how this exemplar is analysed. It does not establish whether quantitative and qualitative data would have been collected concurrently or sequentially in a real study.

For the quantitative calculation, the sample mean difference is divided by the standard error of the 24 differences. A two-sided paired-*t* distribution with 23 degrees of freedom supplies the conventional 95% confidence interval and *p* value. This calculation assumes relevant statistical conditions, including independence between students' paired differences. Neither this assumption nor the equivalence of the assessments can be empirically verified from the synthetic files.

## Quantitative results

The mean pre-score is **66.175 points** and the mean post-score is **74.8708 points**. The difference of those two means agrees with the mean of the 24 paired changes, **8.6958 points**. The sample standard deviation of those changes is **5.9836**, with a standard error of **1.2214**. The paired statistic is **t(23) = 7.1195**, with a two-sided **p ≈ 2.98 × 10⁻⁷**. The conventional paired-*t* **95% interval is [6.1692, 11.2225] points**.

Twenty-three constructed students have positive changes. One, M11, changes by **−4.6 points**. The interval and significance calculation characterise the fictional within-record score differences under a statistical model. They do not estimate the causal effect of retrieval practice. A one-group before-and-after comparison does not distinguish the routine from other possible contributors to changed scores.

The original score CSV contained three inconsistent derived differences. M02 had 3.2 in the change column despite scores of 56.2 and 59.3, which yield **3.1**. M16 had 9.8, whereas 62.9 and 72.8 yield **9.9**. M24 had 16.5, whereas 66.1 and 82.5 yield **16.4**. The derived column was corrected without changing either original score. The stored analysis already agreed with differences recalculated from the original pre- and post-scores.

## Qualitative excerpts and linked comparison

The six excerpts offer different descriptions of what retrieval practice felt like and how students said they approached their work. Their supplied initial codes can be provisionally gathered around noticing gaps, reconstructing or changing preparation, and limits in perceived usefulness. Those groupings are interpretive aids for this instructional example. Six brief excerpts do not establish how prevalent any experience is or provide grounds for a claim of thematic saturation.

The joint display retains the original fictional words and connects them only to scores belonging to the same identifier.

| Student | Score change | Supplied initial code | Exact synthetic interview excerpt |
| --- | ---: | --- | --- |
| M03 | +17.1 | metacognitive diagnosis | “The short recall at the start made me notice what I actually could not explain.” |
| M07 | +16.2 | connection/reconstruction | “I remembered definitions, but the best part was having to connect two ideas without notes.” |
| M09 | +12.9 | burden when content felt mastered | “Some days it felt repetitive when I already knew the topic.” |
| M12 | +13.8 | feedback-guided strategy change | “Checking my answer immediately helped me change how I studied that night.” |
| M18 | +9.1 | limited transfer confidence | “The quiz score went up, but I still felt unsure when the question looked different.” |
| M21 | +14.5 | retrieval-oriented preparation | “I started reviewing by trying to answer first instead of rereading everything.” |

M03's comment concerns difficulty explaining material, while M07 describes connecting ideas without notes. Those accounts are compatible with students using retrieval to examine their understanding. M12 and M21 describe subsequent preparation choices. The excerpts do not demonstrate that these reported experiences produced the corresponding score differences, nor do they allow us to establish the order of study behaviour and assessment changes beyond what each fictional statement says.

Two other excerpts complicate a uniformly favourable reading. M09 finds the routine repetitive at times, despite a 12.9-point increase. M18 describes uncertainty when a question appears in a different form, despite an increase of 9.1 points. The records distinguish an increased score from a student's confidence about applying knowledge in another format. They cannot tell us whether that student actually transferred knowledge successfully or unsuccessfully to another task.

## Mixed-methods integration and limitations

Linking cases gives the numerical pattern a more textured interpretation. Some accounts describe noticing weaknesses or changing preparation, while others express friction or continuing uncertainty. The latter accounts belong to students whose recorded scores increased, illustrating how positive score change and qualified experience can coexist.

The composition of the interview subset places a substantial limit on this integration. **All six excerpt-linked students have positive changes**, with an average increase of **13.93 points**. The remaining 18 students average **6.95 points**, and the only negative-change student, M11, has no interview record. The files do not explain how the subset was selected. Consequently, the excerpts neither represent the full distribution nor explain the decline. No inference about M11's views is warranted.

The quantitative interpretation is also constrained by absent information about the assessment instruments, timing, teaching conditions and participant selection. A control group and clearer documentation could help a real study evaluate competing explanations. On the qualitative side, full interviews, a documented sampling rationale and an inspectable coding process would permit a more defensible analysis. Even when both strands are connected by identifier, a student's retrospective description cannot identify a causal mechanism for a score change.

## Conclusion

The constructed scores show a mean positive paired difference, while six linked fictional accounts offer varied descriptions of retrieval and preparation. The mixed-methods contribution is the visibility of those different forms of evidence alongside one another, including reservations that a positive average cannot express. Because the example has no real participants, documented intervention protocol, comparative group or complete interview record, its findings remain instructional illustrations rather than empirical evidence for the effectiveness of retrieval practice.
